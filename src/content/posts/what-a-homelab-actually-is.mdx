<Eli5>
A homelab is a kitchen, not a restaurant.

A restaurant has to serve customers: uptime matters, the menu is fixed,
and experiments happen carefully if at all. A kitchen at home exists so
you can try things — burn a dish, throw it out, try again. Nobody is
waiting on it.

The moment you treat your homelab like a restaurant — depending on it,
promising it to your family, putting anything on it you cannot lose —
you have built a second job, not a lab.
</Eli5>

<WhyItMatters>
This one distinction decides every purchase. Restaurants need redundancy,
new hardware, and support contracts. Kitchens need counter space and a
tolerance for mess. Most beginner overspending is restaurant equipment
bought for a kitchen.
</WhyItMatters>

## What a lab is for

Three things, in honest order:

1. **Learning by breaking.** You do not understand a system until you
   have broken it and put it back. A lab is the only place that is free.
2. **Running things you want to own.** Local AI models, media, backups,
   ad-blocking — services where the trade is your electricity for your
   privacy.
3. **Rehearsal.** The skills transfer. Virtualisation, networking,
   storage, and recovery are the same muscles at work, at a scale where
   mistakes cost minutes instead of incidents.

## What a lab is not for

Anything with an audience. The day something matters — the family photo
archive, the shared calendar — it stops being an experiment and starts
needing backups, monitoring, and boring stability. That is fine, but
know which machine wears which hat. Mixing them is how labs die: you
stop experimenting because you are afraid of breaking the thing that
matters.

<WhatILearned>
Decide what the lab is allowed to break before you buy anything. The
answer sizes the whole project — and it is usually "everything on it,"
which means you need far less hardware than the shopping lists say.
</WhatILearned>
