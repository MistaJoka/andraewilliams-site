<Tldr>
Buy one used mini-PC, run it for three months, and let the workloads you
actually run — not the ones you imagine — write the second shopping list.
</Tldr>

## The trap: used enterprise gear

The classic advice is a used rack server, because the price-per-spec
looks unbeatable. The specs are real. So is everything the listing does
not say: it idles at the power draw of a space heater, it sounds like
one too, and it teaches you server hardware — which is probably not what
you set out to learn.

You wanted to learn virtualisation, networking, and services. A machine
that is expensive to leave on is a machine you turn off, and a lab that
is off teaches nothing.

## The sweet spot: one used mini-PC

An office-surplus mini-PC — the small business desktops sold by the
thousand off corporate leases — runs a real hypervisor and a dozen light
services, idles under a light bulb, and sits silently on a shelf. That
is the whole starter list:

- **CPU:** whatever the mini-PC came with. It is enough.
- **RAM:** the one spec worth maxing. Virtualisation eats RAM long
  before CPU. 32GB if the price is close.
- **Storage:** one SSD. Redundancy comes later, when something is worth
  protecting.
- **Network:** the port on the back. Managed switches are a part-five
  problem, not a part-one problem.

## What about GPUs?

Only if local AI is the actual goal — it is the one workload that
genuinely needs one, and it changes the build completely: a mini-PC has
no room, no power, and no cooling for it. That fork in the road gets its
own part later in this series. Do not buy the GPU first and let it
dictate everything else unless you already know that is the point.

<FailureModes>
- **Speccing for imagined workloads.** The Kubernetes cluster you might
  run someday keeps not existing while the RAM you bought idles.
- **Buying two of everything for redundancy.** A lab that is allowed to
  break does not need a failover pair. That is restaurant thinking.
- **The empty rack.** Buying the rack before the machines is furniture
  shopping. The shelf was fine.
</FailureModes>

<WhatILearned>
Three months of real usage produces a better hardware plan than any
amount of research, because it replaces guesses about workloads with a
process list you can read.
</WhatILearned>
