Every disputed decision gets the same post-fight complaint: "he landed
more punches." Often true, and often not what the round was scored on —
because the scoring criteria, in order, are effective striking and
grappling, then ring control, then aggression. Volume alone is nowhere
on that list.

## What "effective" actually means

Ten low kicks that don't change the opponent's output score less than
three punches that visibly hurt them. Judges are scoring damage and
positional consequence, not activity — a fighter circling and popping
jabs all round can lose a round to someone who landed one clean counter
that staggered them, because "effective" means the strike changed
something about the fight, not just that it landed.

## Why control outscores volume when it's close

Time spent in a dominant position — back control, full mount, pressed
against the cage dictating movement — counts even in the absence of
finishing damage, because it demonstrates who was making the other
fighter's decisions for them. A fighter landing more strikes off their
back, looking up, is usually losing that exchange on the scorecard even
if the strike count favors them, because the position itself is the
losing signal.

## Where fighters and judges actually disagree

Fighters often feel a round based on who was hurt more. Judges score it
based on who was in control of the round's structure — and those are
frequently different fighters. The fighter who spent three minutes
pressing forward, landing combinations, and controlling the center of
the cage can lose the "who hurt who" read and still win the round on
paper, because effective aggression and control are explicitly weighted
criteria, not afterthoughts to raw damage.
