The instinct is that fast motion needs a fast camera — whip pans, rapid
cuts, motion blur streaking across the frame. The techniques that
actually sell speed most convincingly do close to the opposite: they
slow everything down around one instant, and let your eye supply the
motion the frame isn't drawing.

## The smear frame

A punch connecting isn't drawn as a clean fist mid-swing — it's drawn as
a warped, stretched shape smeared across the space the fist traveled
through, held for a single frame. It's not what a punch looks like; it's
what a punch looks like to a camera shutter too slow to freeze it, which
your visual system already has a lifetime of experience translating back
into "that moved very fast." The smear does the speed's work, not the
frame rate.

## The held frame

Counter-intuitively, the most common technique for selling a fast strike
is holding a *single* drawn pose for several frames — impact, recoil, or
a mid-motion extreme — while everything else in the scene keeps moving
at normal speed around it. The stillness against the surrounding motion
reads as "faster than the frame rate can even show you," because a
genuinely fast action photographed normally would blur past recognition,
and a crisp, static pose implies the camera couldn't keep up either.

## Why the camera staying still is the actual trick

A camera that pans to follow fast motion is honestly depicting speed and
your eye tracks it at a comfortable, readable pace — the opposite of
what fast motion actually feels like to witness. Keeping the camera
locked and forcing the ACTION to violate your expectation of continuity
— a character simply isn't where they were a frame ago, no in-between
shown — makes your own eye do the work of "catching up," which is a much
closer simulation of what watching something genuinely too fast to
track actually feels like.

The transferable idea: don't animate speed by moving more. Animate it by
making the viewer's eye do the moving, and hold everything else still
enough to notice the gap.
