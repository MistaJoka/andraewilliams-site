## What it looks like

A home kept at standard (HME·01) is a stage with nobody on it. Hospitality is what the space is for. The savage's door opens easily, often, and without a three-day warning required to make it presentable. A guest bed or sofa is always made up. A guest toothbrush, towel, and phone charger live in a known spot, stocked before they are needed, not scrambled for at 9 p.m.

The savage initiates. There is a standing hosting cadence — a meal at home, at minimum monthly — on the calendar like any other fixed routine (HME·02). Someone dropping by unannounced gets fed a real meal from what is already in the house, because the pantry is built for exactly that (HME·06). The savage remembers what people drink, what they cannot eat, and what they talked about last time, and uses it without being asked.

Hosting is full attention, not background noise. The phone is away. The host is not disappearing into the kitchen while a guest sits alone with nothing to do — prep happens before the door opens, or the guest is drawn into it. Arrival is met at the door; departure is walked to the door. Both ends of a visit are handled on purpose. None of this requires money or a large home — it requires the same discipline this whole domain runs on, pointed at another person instead of a checklist.

## The gap

The gap looks like a home that is clean but has never once had a guest in it. It looks like "the place isn't ready" as a permanent excuse, used long after the place actually is. It looks like waiting to be invited over rather than ever inviting. It looks like a guest given a tour of clutter, or left standing in a hallway not knowing where to sit. It looks like a phone checked at the table, hosting saved for one big occasion a year and silence in between, and a fridge with nothing to offer the friend who turns up without calling first.

## How to get there

- Stock the guest kit this week: spare toothbrush, towel, charger, one specific place they live (HME·01). Check it before every stay.
- Put a hosting cadence on the calendar — one meal at home, monthly minimum. Treat it like the weekly reset: non-negotiable, not mood-dependent.
- Build one "whoever shows up" meal from pantry staples (HME·06) that needs no shopping and no notice.
- Practice presence: phone in another room while a guest is in the house. Prep before they arrive, not while they wait.
- Keep a short list of the people you host most — drink, diet, last conversation — and glance at it before they come.

## Measures

- Guests hosted at home in the last ninety days: count, at least one.
- Guest kit complete and checked: spare bed or sofa ready, toiletries, charger present. Yes or no.
- Could feed an unannounced guest a real meal from what is in the house right now. Yes or no.
- Standing hosting cadence exists on the calendar, adherence over the last quarter: logged.
- The last three guests hosted: their drink or dietary note recalled without asking. Yes or no.

## Canon

- **The Art of Gathering**, Priya Parker — the modern case for hosting as a deliberate craft, not an accident of good manners.
- **Setting the Table**, Danny Meyer — hospitality as anticipating a need before it is spoken, applied here at home instead of a restaurant.
- **The Book of Tea**, Okakura Kakuzō — the older, quieter case for hospitality as a form of full attention given to another person.
- **The Odyssey**, Homer — the oldest surviving account of hospitality as a moral obligation, not an optional nicety.
