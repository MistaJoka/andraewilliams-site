## What it looks like

The savage does not rise to the level of the emergency; the savage drops to the level of their training. The first action in any crisis is the same: one slow exhale, scan the scene, name the worst thing happening, act on that. This has been rehearsed enough that it runs when the hands shake.

Decision-making under pressure follows a loop the savage can say aloud: observe, decide, act, reassess. Good enough now beats perfect in a minute. Bystanders are given jobs by pointing and naming — "You, call the ambulance." The savage speaks in short, specific sentences and does not raise their voice unless volume is the point.

The body is managed deliberately. Box breathing, a four-count in and out, drops the heart rate. A tactical pause of three seconds before any irreversible action is the rule. The savage knows the feel of their own adrenaline — tunnel vision, time distortion, loud hands — and treats it as data, not command.

Afterwards, the savage debriefs: what happened, what was decided, what would be done differently. Then rests. Composure is spent and replenished; the savage tracks the balance.

## The gap

The gap is freezing. It is the person who screams, the person who films, the person who argues about whose fault it is while the water rises. It is making the first loud suggestion the decision. It is never having been tested, and assuming that means passing.

## How to get there

- Train in conditions that produce stress: first aid scenarios (SRV·01), sparring (SRV·06), cold water, public speaking, timed drills. Seek the feeling on purpose so it becomes familiar.
- Practise box breathing daily for five minutes until it is automatic, then use it the instant the heart rate climbs.
- Memorise one decision loop. Rehearse it on small emergencies — a burst pipe, a lost child in a shop, a car that will not start — by saying each step aloud.
- Take a course that puts you in simulated emergencies with an instructor: wilderness first aid, an emergency response volunteer program, or a community emergency response team.
- Debrief every incident, large or small, in writing within 24 hours.

## Measures

- Simulated-emergency training (scenario-based, instructor-led) completed within the last 24 months, dated.
- Daily breathing practice logged for the last 30 days.
- Decision loop recited from memory on demand: yes/no.
- Resting heart rate returns to within 20 bpm of baseline within 3 minutes of maximal effort, tested in the last 90 days.
- Written debrief exists for every real incident in the last 12 months.

## Canon

- **Thinking, Fast and Slow**, Daniel Kahneman — the science behind fast, biased thinking and how to override it under pressure.
- **Sources of Power**, Gary Klein — the research behind how experienced people make good decisions in seconds, not committees.
- **Man's Search for Meaning**, Viktor Frankl — the classic account of composure and purpose under the most extreme stress imaginable.
- **Breath**, James Nestor — the science behind breathing techniques, including the box breathing this standard trains daily.
