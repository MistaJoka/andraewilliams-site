## What it looks like

The savage runs on Jeff Cooper's color code as a permanent baseline, not a special mode for dangerous places. Condition White — oblivious, phone-down, headphones-in — is never the default. Condition Yellow is: relaxed, unhurried, and quietly aware of who and what is around. It costs nothing and it is always on.

Every environment gets read within seconds of entering it. Where are the exits. Who is watching whom. What is the baseline behavior of this place — the normal rhythm of a train platform, a car park, a bar at 11 p.m. — and what, if anything, breaks that pattern. A stranger moving against the grain, a conversation that goes quiet when you pass, a car that circles twice: these get noticed and logged, not necessarily acted on.

Reading people is a specific skill, not a vibe. Posture, proxemics, hands, and the gap between what someone is saying and how their body is saying it. The savage trusts a bad feeling immediately and investigates it calmly — distance first, questions later — because intuition is pattern recognition running faster than conscious thought (see SRV·06).

The goal, always, is avoidance. Awareness that does not lead anywhere but noticing is unused. The savage crosses the street, changes cars, leaves early, or simply does not go — quietly, before anything happens, and without ever needing to explain why. Nothing in this standard is about winning an encounter. It is about not having one.

## The gap

The gap is condition white as a lifestyle: earbuds in, eyes on a screen, back to the room. It is noticing a problem only once it is already close enough to touch. It is the person who felt something was wrong and stayed anyway because leaving felt awkward. It is confusing vigilance with paranoia and doing neither well — jumpy in a quiet café, oblivious in a genuinely off situation.

## How to get there

- Adopt Condition Yellow as the resting state. On entering any space, take three seconds: exits, people, baseline. Make it a reflex, the way SRV·06 drills exits and SRV·07 drills the breathing reset.
- Practice baseline-and-anomaly reading somewhere low-stakes: a café, a station. Sit for ten minutes and simply note what "normal" looks like there, then what would break it.
- Sit facing the door. Every time, without making it a performance.
- Keep the phone in a pocket in transit — on the street, on transport, in a car park. Eyes up.
- Log every gut-feeling moment, however small, for a month: what triggered it, what you did. Review the pattern.
- Practice the quiet exit: leaving a situation early, without confrontation or explanation, as a default reflex rather than a last resort.

## Measures

- Exits and one anomaly named within 10 seconds of entering an unfamiliar room, tested by a companion: yes/no.
- Phone stays pocketed on foot and on transport: self-audited weekly, logged for the last 90 days.
- Gut-feeling log maintained for at least 30 consecutive days, with an action recorded against every entry.
- Baseline-and-anomaly practice session completed monthly, dated.
- Can describe the four Cooper color code conditions from memory and state today's default: yes/no.

## Canon

- **The Gift of Fear**, Gavin de Becker — the foundational case for trusting instinct and reading pre-incident indicators before anything happens.
- **Left of Bang**, Patrick Van Horne & Jason A. Riley — the baseline-and-anomaly method for reading environments and people, adapted here for everyday awareness rather than a deployed setting.
- **The Unthinkable**, Amanda Ripley — the psychology of how ordinary people notice, or fail to notice, danger before and during a crisis.
- **Spy the Lie**, Philip Houston, Susan Carnicero & Don Tennant — trained observation of behavior and deception cues that sharpens the same reading-people skill this standard requires.
