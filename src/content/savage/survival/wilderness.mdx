## What it looks like

The savage can make fire without a lighter. A ferro rod is the standing skill — struck onto prepared tinder in under a minute, wet weather included — and friction fire (bow drill or hand drill) has been tried enough times to know it is possible, even if it is not relied on. Fire craft extends past ignition: reading wood, building a structure that catches, and keeping a fire going all night in the rain with what was gathered before dark.

Shelter is judged by the conditions, not the gear list. Given a tarp and cordage, the savage pitches a shelter that sheds rain and wind in under fifteen minutes. Given nothing, the savage builds a debris hut or lean-to that holds body heat through a cold night. Site selection — drainage, wind, deadfall overhead — is second nature.

Water is found, then made safe. The savage can identify likely sources by reading terrain and vegetation, and knows three purification methods cold: a rolling boil, a mechanical filter, and a chemical or UV backup — plus which to reach for when. Water is never assumed safe because it looks clear.

Bushcraft is the layer underneath all three: a fixed-blade knife kept sharp and used safely, cordage and knots that hold (bowline, clove hitch, taut-line), firewood processed by hand (baton and feather stick), and weather read from sky and wind without an app. The savage moves through wild terrain leaving no trace and moves confidently because the terrain and the weather (SRV·03) are read, not guessed at.

## The gap

The gap is a phone with 4% battery and a lighter that got wet. It is a "camping trip" that never left cell signal or a propane stove. It is drinking straight from a stream because it looked clean, or standing in the rain because the tarp went up wrong the one time it mattered. It is owning a knife that has never been sharpened and never left the drawer.

## How to get there

- Take a weekend bushcraft or wilderness skills course with a qualified instructor. Repeat annually until the basics are automatic.
- Practice ferro-rod fire lighting monthly, in dry conditions first, then wet. Time it and log it.
- Attempt friction fire at least once with instruction, so the theory has been tested by hand.
- Pitch a tarp shelter from cold, timed, until it is reliably under fifteen minutes. Build one debris shelter and sleep a supervised night in it.
- Practice all three water purification methods on an actual water source, not tap water, under instruction the first time.
- Learn five knots cold and drill them one-handed, gloved, in the dark.
- Carry and use a fixed-blade knife regularly enough that feather sticks and batoning are unremarkable, not a party trick.

## Measures

- Ferro-rod fire to sustained flame: under 60 seconds in dry conditions, tested and dated within the last 12 months.
- Fire built and maintained overnight in wet or cold conditions: completed and dated within the last 24 months.
- Tarp shelter pitched from cold: under 15 minutes, timed within the last 12 months.
- One supervised night in a self-built debris or natural shelter: completed, dated.
- All three water purification methods demonstrated on a real source within the last 12 months.
- Five core knots tied correctly, one-handed, without looking: yes/no, tested by someone else.

## Canon

- **SAS Survival Handbook**, John "Lofty" Wiseman — the widely used comprehensive reference for fire, shelter, water, and field survival skills.
- **Bushcraft**, Mors Kochanski — the foundational modern bushcraft text on tool use, fire craft, and living off what the land provides.
- **Bushcraft 101**, Dave Canterbury — a practical, modern field guide to the core wilderness skills this standard requires.
- **The Complete Walker**, Colin Fletcher — the classic reference for self-sufficient travel and camping in the outdoors.
