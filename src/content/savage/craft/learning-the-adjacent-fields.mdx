## What it looks like

The savage's core subject (see CRF·01) sits inside a map of neighbours, and they can work in each. A builder who understands the business they build for. An analyst who can ship the model, not just describe it. A tradesperson who reads the engineering drawing and the contract. Range is not a hobby; it is what makes the savage the person called when the problem does not fit one department.

Learning is scheduled like training. A fixed block of hours every week, defended against everything, spent on one adjacent field at a time until it is usable. Usable means a real artefact was produced in it — a working thing, a delivered piece of analysis, a job done — not a course certificate. One new field per year, to working level, is the pace. A second career's worth of skill every decade.

The savage also learns backward: the history of their craft, the failed approaches, the reasons the current practice won. Old ideas come back, and the savage recognises them when they do.

## The gap

The gap looks like a deep well with no surface — expert in one thing, helpless a metre to the left. It looks like a bookshelf of unread books and a list of "should learn" that has not changed in three years. It looks like a course completed and nothing built. It looks like contempt for the neighbouring discipline, which is usually fear of it.

## How to get there

- Draw the map: the core subject in the middle, the four to six fields it touches around it. Rank them by how often they block you.
- Pick one. Block five hours a week for it, on the calendar, for six months.
- Learn by producing. Set a concrete artefact as the finish line before the first hour is spent.
- Find the canonical text and the best practitioner in the field. Read the first; talk to the second.
- Ship the artefact in public (see CRF·06) and take the correction.
- Read one history of the craft every year.

## Measures

- Adjacent-field map written down, dated, ranked.
- Learning hours logged: at least 200 in the last twelve months, on-calendar and protected.
- One adjacent field reached working level in the last year, proven by a finished, dated artefact.
- Count of adjacent fields at working level: two or more, each with an artefact to show.
- One history of the craft read in the last twelve months, with notes.

## Canon

- **Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World**, David Epstein — the direct case for the breadth this standard demands, against the cult of early specialization.
- **Where Good Ideas Come From**, Steven Johnson — the "adjacent possible": why the next breakthrough lives one field over, not deeper in the one you know.
- **The Medici Effect**, Frans Johansson — what happens at the intersection of fields, and why the savage who works there gets called first.
- **A Technique for Producing Ideas**, James Webb Young — the short classic on combining knowledge from separate domains into something new.
