## What it looks like

The savage has a written standard and every piece of work meets it or does not ship. The standard is the same for a client and for a personal experiment, because quality is a habit, not a performance. Correct first. Then clear. Then fast. In that order, always.

Verification is evidence, never assertion. Tests that ran, numbers that reconciled, a checklist with every box ticked by hand, a second pair of eyes on anything irreversible. "It works" is not a sentence the savage says; they show the output. When the work is wrong, the standard says so before the customer does, because the savage reviews their own work as an adversary would.

Quality is also restraint: no untested change stacked on an unverified one, no scope smuggled in, no clever solution where a plain one would do. The work is as simple as the problem allows and no simpler.

## The gap

The gap looks like "it worked on my machine." It looks like green tests that were weakened until they passed. It looks like a report with a number nobody reconciled, shipped because the deadline was today. It looks like different standards for the work people see and the work they do not. It looks like a defect found by the customer that a five-minute check would have caught.

## How to get there

- Write the standard on one page: what every deliverable must have before it leaves. Definition of correct, definition of verified, definition of documented. Print it.
- Verify before claiming. Every "done" is accompanied by its evidence — output, test run, reconciliation, sign-off.
- Review own work cold: sleep on anything irreversible, then read it as the harshest critic you know.
- Run a pre-mortem on every significant deliverable: "this failed — why?" Fix the top answer before shipping.
- Keep a defect log: every error that escaped, its root cause, and the check added so it cannot recur.
- Never fake green. If the only path to passing is weakening the check, that is the report, not a fix.

## Measures

- One-page written quality standard exists and was updated within the last twelve months.
- Every item in the shipped ledger (see CRF·02) for the last quarter has evidence attached: yes or no.
- Defect log exists; escaped defects in the last year counted, each with root cause and a permanent check.
- Escaped-defect count trending down year on year.
- Zero instances in the last year of a test, check, or assertion weakened to pass.

## Canon

- **The Checklist Manifesto**, Atul Gawande — the evidence that written checklists, not expertise alone, are what cut error rates in high-stakes work.
- **Extreme Programming Explained**, Kent Beck — the origin of continuous verification and testing as a discipline rather than an afterthought.
- **Thinking, Fast and Slow**, Daniel Kahneman — why self-review needs adversarial distance; the mind that made the error cannot be trusted to find it.
- **Toyota Production System**, Taiichi Ohno — the source of stopping the line for a defect instead of shipping it downstream.
