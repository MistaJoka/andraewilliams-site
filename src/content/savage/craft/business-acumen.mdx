## What it looks like

The savage knows what their craft costs to produce and what it is worth, and never confuses the two. Every hour, every deliverable, every engagement has a number attached: time cost, overhead, price, and what is left after both. Skill without this number is a hobby that happens to pay sometimes.

Pricing is deliberate. It is set from cost-plus-margin and market value, not guessed from what feels comfortable to ask out loud. The rate is revisited on a schedule, not when resentment finally forces the issue. The savage can state, without checking, their effective hourly rate on their last three engagements — after taxes, after overhead, after the unpaid hours nobody bills.

They understand the difference between revenue and profit, between busy and paid, between a full calendar and a solvent one. Cash flow is watched, not discovered after the fact. They know which of their offerings makes money and which is a loss leader kept on purpose, and can name which is which without hesitation.

## The gap

The gap looks like a skilled person who has never priced their own hour. It looks like "exposure" accepted as payment, discounts given because saying a number out loud is uncomfortable, and a rate that has not moved in three years while costs have moved plenty. It looks like being fully booked and broke — a calendar that says success and a bank balance that says otherwise. It looks like scope creep absorbed silently because the relationship matters more than the invoice, every time, forever, until it doesn't.

## How to get there

- Calculate the real cost of an hour of your work: overhead, taxes, unbillable time, tools, insurance. Divide annual cost of doing business by billable hours. Know the number.
- Set price from cost-plus-margin and market comparables, not from what feels safe to ask. Write the number down before the conversation, not during it.
- Track margin on every engagement or product line. Kill or reprice anything that runs at a loss without a deliberate, stated reason.
- Review rates on a fixed schedule — annually at minimum — and raise them when the calculation says to, not when courage finally arrives.
- Separate revenue from profit in every report you read about your own work. A full pipeline (see CRF·02) that does not convert to margin is a warning, not a win.
- Read your own numbers monthly: what came in, what it cost to deliver, what is left. Thirty minutes, same day every month, no excuses.

## Measures

- Effective hourly rate after overhead and unbillable time, calculated and dated within the last ninety days.
- Written price list or rate card exists, reviewed at least once in the last twelve months.
- Profit margin known for every active offering or engagement type: yes or no.
- Monthly numbers review completed in at least ten of the last twelve months, dated.
- Zero engagements accepted in the last twelve months at a knowingly negative margin without a stated strategic reason.

## Canon

- **Company of One**, Paul Jarvis — the case for building a business sized to the person running it, not scaled past what the economics justify.
- **Profit First**, Mike Michalowicz — a mechanical system for seeing profit before expenses eat it, built for people who avoid their own numbers.
- **Value-Based Fees**, Alan Weiss — the argument and method for pricing on value delivered instead of hours worked.
- **The E-Myth Revisited**, Michael E. Gerber — why being good at the craft and running the business of the craft are two different skills, and why most people only ever learn the first.
