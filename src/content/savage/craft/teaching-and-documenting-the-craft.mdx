## What it looks like

The savage leaves a trail a stranger can follow. Every system they build has a document that lets someone else run it. Every hard-won lesson becomes a written rule. Every procedure they perform more than once has a runbook: the exact steps, the failure modes, the way back. If the savage vanished tonight, the work would keep running on what they wrote.

Teaching is the test of understanding. The savage explains the craft regularly — to juniors, peers, clients, readers — and treats every point of confusion in the listener as a gap in their own model. They can teach the fundamentals from a blank page in their own words, and they can do it at three levels: the five-minute version, the one-hour version, and the version that produces a practitioner.

Documentation is written for the reader who is tired, new, and in a hurry. Short, correct, current. A document that has drifted from reality is worse than none, so the savage treats docs as part of the deliverable, updated in the same change that altered the thing.

## The gap

The gap looks like "just ask me." It looks like a system only one person can deploy and a procedure that lives in that person's head. It looks like documentation written once and never touched as the thing changed beneath it. It looks like the expert who cannot explain the basics because they forgot what it was like not to know them. It looks like knowledge that dies with the job change.

## How to get there

- Runbook everything operational: one page per procedure, tested by handing it to someone else and watching them follow it without help.
- Write the explanation at the moment of understanding. The day you finally get it is the day you can best explain it to the person you were yesterday.
- Teach on a schedule: one session a month, any format — a junior, a talk, a written guide, a recorded walkthrough.
- Update docs in the same change as the code, the process, or the tool. No separate "docs later" task exists.
- Keep a personal handbook: how you work, what you believe about the craft, the rules you have paid for. Revise it yearly.
- Build people who outgrow you. Count them.

## Measures

- Every operational system or recurring procedure has a runbook, and each was followed successfully by someone else within the last twelve months.
- Teaching sessions delivered in the last year: twelve or more, dated.
- Documentation drift audit done quarterly; stale documents found and fixed, counted.
- Personal craft handbook exists, revised within the last twelve months.
- Named people who learned a working skill from the savage and now practise it independently: three or more, listed.

## Canon

- **On Writing Well**, William Zinsser — the classic standard for clear, human nonfiction prose that any documentation should meet.
- **Docs for Developers**, Jared Bhatti, Zachariah Goldberg, Ted Kaplan, Amy Vernon, Jen Lambourne & David Nunez — a modern field guide to writing documentation people actually use.
- **The Art of Explanation**, Lee LeFever — how to find the point of confusion in a listener and fix the explanation, not the listener.
- **The Elements of Style**, William Strunk Jr. & E.B. White — the short, brutal rulebook behind writing that a tired stranger can follow.
