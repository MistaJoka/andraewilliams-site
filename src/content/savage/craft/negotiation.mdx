## What it looks like

The savage negotiates before the work starts, not during the slip. Scope, price, timeline, and definition of done are agreed in writing before the first hour is spent, and every change after that gets a new conversation, not a silent absorption. "Can you just—" is answered with a scope change, a price change, or a no. Never with a shrug.

Stakeholders are managed like a system with inputs and expectations, not left to discover the outcome at the deadline. The savage tells people what they need to hear on a schedule, before they have to ask — status, risk, and bad news early, not buried until it can't be avoided. Bad news travels faster from the savage than from anyone else on the project.

Saying no is a practiced skill, not an occasional act of courage. The savage can decline a request, a deadline, or a client outright, cleanly, with the reason stated and an alternative offered where one exists. They know the difference between a request they can meet and one they are being pressured to accept, and they never let urgency substitute for a real conversation about tradeoffs (see CRF·04).

## The gap

The gap looks like a scope of work agreed in a hallway and remembered differently by both sides later. It looks like every "small ask" absorbed for free until the job priced at forty hours quietly became two hundred. It looks like silence when a deadline is clearly at risk, followed by a manufactured surprise when it arrives. It looks like saying yes to avoid a hard conversation and resenting the person for the yes they were given. It looks like a stakeholder update that only happens when things are already going badly.

## How to get there

- Put scope, price, timeline, and done-definition in writing before any work starts. No exceptions, including for people you trust.
- Build a change-order habit: any request outside written scope gets a new price, a new date, or a stated no — said out loud, in the moment, not absorbed silently.
- Schedule stakeholder updates before they are asked for — weekly or at each milestone, whichever is shorter. Bad news goes in the first line, not the last.
- Practice saying no as a sentence, not a negotiation: state it, give the one-line reason, offer the alternative if one exists, stop talking.
- Walk away from at least one engagement a year that fails your own terms on price, scope, or client behavior. If none failed, the terms are not real.
- After every negotiation, write down what was agreed and send it to the other side within 24 hours. Silence is not agreement.

## Measures

- Every active engagement has a written, signed scope, price, and done-definition: yes or no.
- Change orders issued in the last twelve months versus scope creep absorbed silently: majority issued, tracked.
- Stakeholder updates sent on schedule, last quarter: 90% or higher, with bad news disclosed within 48 hours of being known.
- At least one engagement declined or exited on principle in the last twelve months, logged with reason.
- Written confirmation sent within 24 hours after 100% of negotiations in the last quarter.

## Canon

- **Getting to Yes**, Roger Fisher & William Ury — the foundational text on principled negotiation: interests over positions, options before commitment.
- **Never Split the Difference**, Chris Voss — tactical, field-tested negotiation technique from a hostage negotiator, built for real leverage asymmetries.
- **Crucial Conversations**, Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan & Al Switzler — the method for the hard conversation when stakes and emotion are both high.
- **The Trusted Advisor**, David H. Maister, Charles H. Green & Robert M. Galford — how to manage a client relationship so the hard conversations get easier, not harder, over time.
