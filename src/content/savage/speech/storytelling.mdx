## What it looks like

The savage treats story as a tool, not decoration. Every message that matters — a pitch, a toast, a piece of hard feedback, a vision for a team — gets built with structure: a specific person, a concrete stake, an obstacle, a turn, and a change that means something. Abstractions get replaced with scenes: not "we struggled early on" but "eighteen months in, payroll bounced twice in the same quarter."

The savage knows five or six core stories cold — an origin, a failure, a turning point, a mentor, a near-miss — and can deploy the right one in under three minutes with no warm-up, the same readiness demanded of any talk in SPK·04. Detail is specific and sensory but never padded; a story gets cut the same way any piece of writing does, sentence by sentence, until only what earns its place remains (SPK·03). The ending lands on the change itself, not on "and that's basically it."

Story is used to make the abstract legible: a strategy becomes an account of the one customer it exists to help; a value becomes the day it actually cost something. The savage can find the story inside someone else's data or decision within a minute of hearing it, because that is how people remember and act — on the account of what happened, not on the number attached to it.

## The gap

The gap is the update that stays in bullet points and abstractions and nobody carries out of the room. It's the pitch that opens with market size instead of the person the product is meant to change. It's a story with no stakes — "so then this happened, and then this happened" — that goes nowhere and takes four minutes doing it. It's the humble-brag dressed up as an anecdote, and the same story told the same flat way regardless of the room. It's having nothing ready when the moment calls for it, and reaching for a slide instead.

## How to get there

- Write your five core stories in full — origin, biggest failure, turning point, a mentor, a near-miss — then cut each by half.
- Structure every one on paper: person, stake, obstacle, turn, change. Any sentence that doesn't serve one of those five gets cut.
- Tell each story out loud to a real person at least once a quarter. Time it — the ceiling is three minutes unless the room asked for more.
- Before any pitch, update, or hard conversation, find the one scene that carries the point and open with it instead of the summary.
- Study story structure directly: break down how a professional storyteller builds a five-minute piece, then rebuild one of your own to match its bones.
- Keep a running log of moments as they happen — one line, dated — so the material exists before the moment that needs it.

## Measures

- Five core stories written down, each under 300 words, dated within the last year.
- A story told live and timed in the last quarter, at three minutes or under, with structure intact: person, stake, obstacle, turn, change.
- The last pitch or major update you gave opened with a specific scene, not a summary — checked against notes or a recording.
- A running story log with dated entries, no gap longer than thirty days across the last six months.

## Canon

- **The Hero with a Thousand Faces**, Joseph Campbell — the underlying shape most durable, memorable stories share.
- **Into the Woods**, John Yorke — a working screenwriter's breakdown of how and why story structure actually functions.
- **Made to Stick**, Chip Heath and Dan Heath — why some ideas and stories are remembered and repeated, and most are not.
- **The Story Factor**, Annette Simmons — story treated explicitly as a leadership and influence tool, not craft for its own sake.
