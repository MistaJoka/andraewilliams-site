## What it looks like

The savage can stand up in front of any group without notes and speak for two minutes or twenty, and the audience remembers the one thing that mattered. The structure is visible: here is the point, here are three reasons, here is what I want from you. Voice reaches the back row without a microphone. Pace is slower than feels natural. Pauses are held long enough to be uncomfortable for the speaker and exactly right for the listener.

Eyes move deliberately across the room and settle on individuals for a full sentence at a time. Hands are still or purposeful. No slides unless a picture does what words cannot; when there are slides, they are pictures and numbers, not the script. The savage finishes under time, every time, and stops on the last sentence rather than trailing into "so, yeah, that's it".

Questions are welcomed, repeated back so the room hears them, and answered in one breath. "I don't know" is a complete answer when it is true.

## The gap

The gap is reading from slides with the back to the room. It is the ten-minute preamble about how the speaker got here. It is mumbling the key number, rushing the ending, running fifteen minutes over and taking questions from nobody because there is no time. It is "can everyone hear me at the back?" asked once and never fixed. It is the toast that rambles and the update with no point. It is refusing the invitation to speak because the heart rate went up.

## How to get there

- Take every invitation to speak. Toasts, eulogies, team updates, panels. Target twelve talks a year minimum.
- Build every talk on one sentence the audience should repeat afterward. Everything else supports it or gets cut.
- Rehearse aloud, standing, timed, at least three full runs. Film one. Watch it at 1.5× speed — the tics show fast.
- Practise impromptu: draw a random topic, speak for two minutes with point–reasons–ask structure. Three times a week until it is automatic.
- Train the voice: diaphragmatic breathing, projection drills, reading aloud ten minutes a day. Speak at 130–150 words per minute; most people rush to 180.
- Join a structured speaking group or hire a coach for one year.

## Measures

- Talk log: date, audience size, length planned vs actual. Twelve or more entries in the last twelve months, every one finished on or under time.
- A filmed talk from the last six months, reviewed, with three specific corrections written down.
- Two-minute impromptu on an unseen topic, recorded this month, with clear point–reasons–ask structure and no fillers.
- Audience can state your main point when asked afterward — verified at least once per quarter.

## Canon

- **Rhetoric**, Aristotle — the foundational text on structuring persuasive, memorable speech.
- **TED Talks: The Official TED Guide to Public Speaking**, Chris Anderson — the modern craft of holding a room and landing one idea.
- **The Quick and Easy Way to Effective Speaking**, Dale Carnegie — classic drills for confident, structured extemporaneous speaking.
- **Confessions of a Public Speaker**, Scott Berkun — practical, unglamorous lessons from a working professional speaker.
