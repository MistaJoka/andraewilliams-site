## What it looks like

The savage prepares for negotiation the way an athlete prepares for competition — every time, even for small stakes. Before a word is spoken there is a stated walk-away point, a written best-alternative-to-a-negotiated-agreement (BATNA), and a real guess at the other side's interests, not just their opening position. Anchors are set deliberately, first only when the savage holds better information than the room does; otherwise the savage lets the other side speak first and reads what it reveals.

Positions get translated into interests before they get argued: not "I want the corner office" but "I need to see the floor to lead it." Every concession is traded, named out loud, and logged — nothing given away to relieve the discomfort of a silence. Calibrated questions — "what makes this hard for you?", "how am I supposed to do that?" — do work that pressure and threats cannot, an application of the same listening discipline as SPK·02. After an offer lands, the savage stops talking; the next person to speak first usually gives something up, and the savage is comfortable letting them.

Persuasion runs on evidence, framing, and reciprocity, never on guilt, manufactured urgency, or force. The savage can state the other side's strongest argument back to them, accurately, before answering it. A close only counts as a close when both sides can repeat the terms the same way days later — read the room first (SPK·07), then negotiate the substance.

## The gap

The gap is walking in with a target number and no floor. It's accepting the first offer to escape an uncomfortable silence, or countering out of ego with no number behind it. It's mistaking volume for leverage, or capitulating to keep the peace and calling it diplomacy. It's "trust me" persuasion that offers no reason, flattery standing in for evidence, and a deadline invented on the spot to force a decision. It's the deal that unravels within a month because one side didn't actually agree — they just stopped objecting.

## How to get there

- Before any negotiation with real stakes, write three lines: your walk-away number, your BATNA, and your best guess at their interests. Every time, no exceptions.
- Practise calibrated questions and mirroring in low-stakes settings — a restaurant bill, a return, a scheduling conflict — until they are reflexive.
- Never state an opening number before hearing theirs, unless you hold demonstrably better information.
- Log every negotiation that matters: what was asked, what was traded, what closed. Review the losses harder than the wins.
- Before making an offer, identify what the other side actually needs — to win publicly, to save face, to move fast — and shape the offer around it, not just the number.
- Study one real negotiation transcript or case a month — a labor deal, a hostage negotiation, a term sheet — and write down the specific moves used.

## Measures

- A negotiation log exists, dated, with a walk-away number and BATNA recorded before at least ten real negotiations in the last year.
- Zero opening offers accepted without a counter in the last twelve months, checked against the log.
- Can state the other side's strongest argument, unprompted and accurately, within one minute, on each of the last three negotiations attempted.
- A negotiated agreement from the last six months that both sides still describe the same way when asked separately.

## Canon

- **Getting to Yes**, Roger Fisher and William Ury — principled negotiation: interests over positions, options generated before decisions are forced.
- **Never Split the Difference**, Chris Voss — calibrated questions, mirroring, and tactical empathy, drawn from hostage negotiation.
- **Influence**, Robert Cialdini — the real psychological levers of persuasion, and how to recognize them being used on you.
- **Bargaining for Advantage**, G. Richard Shell — negotiation style assessment and the discipline of preparing before you sit down.
