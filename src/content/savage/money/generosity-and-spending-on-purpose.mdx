## What it looks like

Money under command is money that leaves on purpose. The savage gives at least ten percent of income, off the top, automated like the savings (see MNY·03), to causes and people chosen deliberately and reviewed annually. Giving is the first transfer after the money arrives, not the leftover at year end. It is also quiet; nobody is told unless telling helps the cause.

Spending follows a written list of what actually matters — five things, no more — and the budget is shaped around them. The savage spends freely, even lavishly, on those five and almost nothing on everything else. Car, clothes, phone, house: each either serves one of the five or is bought at the cheapest level that does the job. Status purchases do not happen, because the savage does not need strangers to read them as wealthy.

There is no impulse spending. Anything above a fixed threshold waits seventy-two hours; anything above a larger one waits thirty days, and most of it is never bought. Large purchases are paid from cash already set aside, never financed (see MNY·05). Time is priced: the savage knows their hourly value and buys back time wherever the trade clears. Money also flows to people — family supported without being asked twice, friends repaid the same day, tips generous.

## The gap

The gap looks like giving what is left, which is nothing. It looks like a closet of things bought to feel better for an afternoon. It looks like a lease chosen for how it reads in a parking lot. It looks like no idea where a third of the month went, a vague unease about whether the money buys anything that lasts, and generosity announced more than practised.

## How to get there

- Write the five priorities tonight. Put them at the top of the ledger (see MNY·01). Every budget category must trace to one or be cut to the minimum.
- Set the giving transfer for the day after payday. Raise it one point per quarter until it reaches ten.
- Choose the recipients once a year, in writing: who, how much, why. Review every January.
- Set two waiting thresholds — three days and thirty days — and keep a list of everything that waited.
- Compute an hourly value of your time. Outsource anything under it that you do not enjoy.
- Audit the last ninety days of variable spend against the five. Anything that traces to nothing is the next cut.
- Pay every personal debt — money or favour — the day it is incurred.

## Measures

- Giving over the trailing twelve months divided by gross income: ten percent or above, automated, recipients listed and dated.
- Written list of five priorities exists; every budget category maps to one or sits at its minimum.
- Thirty-day hold list exists with entries from the last ninety days.
- Zero large purchases financed in the last five years.
- Ninety-day variable spend traced to priorities: ninety percent or above by value.
- Zero personal debts of money or favour older than seven days.
