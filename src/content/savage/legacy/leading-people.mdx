## What it looks like

Leading is not a title. It is what happens when a group of people needs a direction and you supply it, carry the weight of the call, and take the blame when it goes wrong. The savage leads the same way in a kitchen, a crew, a family, or a company: clearly, early, without needing to be liked.

The standard is visible in the people. They know what the goal is and could state it in one sentence. They know what is expected of them this week. They hear bad news from you first and in private, and good news in public. They are never surprised by a performance conversation because the feedback has been continuous. They disagree with you in the room and execute the decision outside it.

You make decisions on the information you have and on time. You own the outcomes of your team's work in full — credit flows down, blame flows up, and nobody under you has ever been thrown under the bus to protect your standing. When someone is not working out, you act inside a quarter, with dignity.

## The gap

The gap is the manager who avoids hard conversations and calls it kindness. It is the leader who is vague so that nothing can be pinned on them, who delegates tasks but never authority, who takes the stage for a win and sends an email for a loss. It is a team that learns what the leader wants by watching their mood. It is the "leader" nobody has ever chosen to follow twice.

## How to get there

- Write the mission of any group you lead in one sentence. Say it until people can repeat it back, then say it again.
- Hold a one-to-one with every direct report at a fixed cadence — weekly or fortnightly — and never cancel it for your own convenience.
- Give feedback within forty-eight hours of the behaviour. Specific, private, and with the expected change stated.
- Decide. A good decision today beats a perfect one in three weeks. Record the reasoning.
- Delegate outcomes, not tasks. Hand over the authority that goes with the responsibility, then stay out of the way.
- Handle underperformance inside ninety days: clear expectations, a dated plan, and a decision at the end.

## Measures

- Every direct report can state the team's goal in one sentence — ask them, quarterly.
- One-to-one cadence kept: fewer than two missed in the last quarter, by your calendar.
- Every open performance issue has a dated plan less than ninety days old.
- At least one person you led before has chosen to work with you again.
- Zero instances in the last year of blame pushed down to a report.

## Canon

- **Extreme Ownership**, Jocko Willink & Leif Babin — the case for total accountability, credit down and blame up, from officers who lived it.
- **The Making of a Manager**, Julie Zhuo — a clear-eyed, modern account of what a manager actually owes the people who report to them.
- **First, Break All the Rules**, Marcus Buckingham & Curt Coffman — the research behind what great managers do differently, drawn from large-scale data.
- **The Five Dysfunctions of a Team**, Patrick Lencioni — why teams fail without trust and accountability, and what a leader has to build first.
