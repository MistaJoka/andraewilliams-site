## What it looks like

Building (see LGC·03) is making something new. Stewardship is different: holding something that already existed before you, in trust, for whoever comes after. A role you inherited, a team you did not hire, a budget, a piece of land, a board seat, a family heirloom, a community institution someone else founded. The savage treats none of it as owned. It is held, on behalf of people not yet named.

It looks like an audit on arrival — condition, obligations, history — written down within the first ninety days, before your own changes blur the baseline. It looks like defining what "better" means in numbers before you start touching anything. It looks like funding maintenance that produces no applause: the unglamorous budget line, the unsexy repair, the institutional memory nobody thanks you for preserving. It looks like respecting what predecessors built well enough not to erase it for the sake of leaving a mark.

It looks like stewardship reaching past your formal scope — the shared kitchen, the commons, the environment, the junior colleague who is not technically yours to develop. And it looks like an honest account at departure: here is what I received, here is what I am leaving, compared side by side.

## The gap

The gap is inheriting something healthy and running it down through neglect or ego. It is treating a borrowed resource as owned — spending down a budget, a reputation, or a piece of land as if there is no one after you. It is asset-stripping: harvesting short-term gain from something you did not build, at the expense of the people who will hold it next. It is skipping maintenance because it is invisible until it fails. It is taking credit for a predecessor's work while letting the thing itself decay. It is leaving a role with no honest account of the state you leave it in.

## How to get there

- On taking any entrusted role or resource, write a baseline audit within ninety days: condition, obligations, history, what is fragile.
- Define "better" in numbers before you start — financial position, physical condition, capability, reputation — so improvement is provable, not asserted.
- Budget and fund maintenance explicitly for anything you steward but did not build. Unglamorous upkeep gets a line item, not leftover attention.
- Keep a running, dated account of condition, visible to whoever might need to step in — not locked in your head.
- Refuse any decision that trades the long-term health of what you steward for your own short-term gain. Name the refusal out loud when it costs you something.
- On departure, write the comparison: baseline versus current state, plain and honest, handed to your successor.

## Measures

- A written baseline exists for every entrusted role or resource you hold, dated within the first ninety days.
- A written state-at-departure comparison exists for every entrusted role you have left.
- At least one numeric measure of condition (financial, physical, or capability) is better at the last audit than at the baseline — name the metric and the number.
- Maintenance or reinvestment funded in the last twelve months for at least one thing you steward but did not build.
- Zero draws against a shared or entrusted resource for personal benefit without disclosure — audited annually.

## Canon

- **A Sand County Almanac**, Aldo Leopold — the land ethic; the classic articulation of stewardship as an obligation to what outlasts you.
- **Stewardship: Choosing Service Over Self-Interest**, Peter Block — the direct case for holding authority as service to an institution rather than ownership of it.
- **The Tragedy of the Commons**, Garrett Hardin — the foundational essay on why shared resources decay without deliberate, accountable custodianship.
- **The Servant as Leader**, Robert K. Greenleaf — the essay that founded servant leadership: authority exercised for those served, not the one holding it.
