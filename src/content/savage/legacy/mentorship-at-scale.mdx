## What it looks like

Teaching one person is a relationship (see LGC·02). Mentorship at scale is a system. The savage does not stop at raising up a capable follower — they raise up other leaders, deliberately, and then get out of the way while those leaders raise up the next layer themselves. The test is not "how many people report to me" but "how many people who once reported to me now lead others well."

It looks like identifying high-potential people early and handing them real authority, not just more tasks — a project to own, a hire to make, a budget to run, a decision that is genuinely theirs to get wrong. It looks like sponsorship: putting their name forward for rooms you are not in, promotions you do not control, credit you could have kept. It looks like a structured pipeline — rotation, stretch roles, a fixed cadence of development conversations — rather than mentorship that happens only when it is convenient.

It looks like tracking the second generation. Not just who you developed, but who they developed. A leader who multiplies leaves a family tree, not a fan base.

## The gap

The gap is the leader who mentors one person at a time forever and never builds a pipeline. It is the manager whose department collapses the moment three key people leave, because "key people" were never given real authority while under them. It is hoarding leadership opportunities — running every important meeting yourself, keeping the interesting decisions for your own desk — while calling it mentorship because you occasionally explain your reasoning. It is being "available" to mentees who are never actually promoted, sponsored, or handed the room.

## How to get there

- Name three to five high-potential people now. For each, define the next real piece of authority you will hand them within ninety days — not a task, a decision that is theirs.
- Build a leadership pipeline with stages: individual contributor, first authority, first team, first successor of their own. Move people through it on purpose, not by accident of tenure.
- Sponsor out loud: nominate, introduce, and put their name on work in front of people who can promote them. Track it like you track any other commitment.
- Run development conversations on a fixed cadence, separate from performance reviews — what they're ready for next, and what's in the way.
- Ask every leader you developed who they are developing. If the answer is nobody, that is the next conversation.
- When you leave a role, count the leaders you leave behind, not the metrics you leave behind.

## Measures

- Named high-potential people with a dated ninety-day authority plan: at least three, reviewed quarterly.
- People you have directly developed into a leadership role — title or scope change — with dates: a number you can produce on request.
- Second-generation leaders — people mentored by someone you mentored — named and counted at least once a year.
- Sponsorship actions logged in the last twelve months: nominations, introductions, public credit, at least one per quarter.
- Of the leaders currently under you, the fraction who could run their function for a full quarter without you: measured, not estimated.

## Canon

- **The Leadership Pipeline**, Ram Charan, Stephen Drotter & James Noel — the standard framework for developing leaders through each transition of authority.
- **Multipliers**, Liz Wiseman — the distinction between leaders who amplify the capability of others and leaders who quietly diminish it.
- **Turn the Ship Around!**, L. David Marquet — a submarine commander's account of deliberately distributing leadership rather than concentrating it in himself.
- **The Leadership Engine**, Noel Tichy — why organisations that outperform over decades do so by systematically producing leaders at every level.
