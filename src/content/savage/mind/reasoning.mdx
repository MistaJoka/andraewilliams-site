## What it looks like

The savage does not argue from analogy, authority, or what everyone knows. A problem is broken down to what is actually true — the physics, the incentives, the arithmetic — and rebuilt from there. When a claim arrives, the first question is "what would have to be true for this to hold," and the second is "what would I expect to see if it were false."

Judgement is probabilistic, never binary. "I think so" is replaced with a number: 70 percent, 95 percent, 10 percent. The numbers are calibrated, which means when the savage says 80 percent, the thing happens about eight times in ten. The savage keeps score.

Bias is treated as a known defect in the instrument, not a character flaw. Confirmation bias, sunk cost, anchoring, availability, the narrative fallacy, motivated reasoning — the savage can name them, knows which ones personally bite hardest, and has procedures that catch them before they cost anything.

The ceiling reads: decisions above a set threshold of consequence are written down before they are made, with the reasoning, the probability estimate, and what would change the call. Six months later they are reviewed against what happened.

## The gap

The gap looks like confidence without a track record. It looks like "I just had a feeling" and "everyone knew." It looks like defending a position harder because it is yours. It looks like never having been measurably wrong because nothing was ever stated precisely enough to be wrong.

## How to get there

- Keep a decision journal. For any choice above your stated threshold: the situation, the options, the probability on each, the reasoning, the review date.
- Forecast. Put numbers on questions that resolve, track a Brier score or equivalent, and drive it down over a year.
- Run a pre-mortem on anything that matters: assume it failed, write the most likely cause, fix that first.
- Steelman before you argue. State the opposing case well enough that its holder would sign it.
- Learn the dozen biases that matter and assign each a tripwire: a question you ask yourself at the moment it usually fires.

## Measures

- Decision journal exists with entries dated within the last thirty days and at least ten reviewed entries in the last year.
- A calibration record of fifty or more resolved forecasts, with the score computed.
- You can write down, from memory, the three biases that most affect you and the tripwire for each.
- In the last twelve months you changed a stated position on evidence, and it is logged with the date.
