## What it looks like

Meditation is attention training, not relaxation, though relaxation sometimes shows up as a side effect. The savage sits on a schedule, on a cushion or a chair, eyes closed or lowered, and does one thing: notices where attention is, notices when it has wandered, and returns it, over and over, without narrating the failure as failure.

The practice has a spine, not just a timer. Breath or another anchor is chosen and held. When thought arrives — and it arrives constantly — it is labelled and released rather than followed or suppressed. Over months the gap between wandering and noticing shrinks. That gap is the entire skill, and it transfers directly into focus, equanimity, and every other standard in this domain (see MND·03, MND·06).

Stillness is trained the way a muscle is trained: past comfort, on purpose. Sessions extend from minutes to an hour. Silent retreats of a day or more are done periodically, not as spiritual tourism but as the equivalent of a max-effort training block — a stress test for a skill practiced daily at lower intensity.

The ceiling reads: a daily sit of twenty minutes minimum, unbroken for years, with sessions of forty-five minutes to an hour multiple times a week and at least one extended retreat or silent day per year. The savage can sit through boredom, physical discomfort, and an unpleasant emotion without reaching for a phone, a snack, or an exit.

## The gap

The gap looks like a meditation app opened eleven times and finished four. It looks like "I tried it, my mind was too busy" — the busy mind is the entire reason for the practice, not evidence it does not work. It looks like meditating only on good days, or only in a retreat setting where it is easy, and never in the ordinary week where it is needed. It looks like confusing a guided audio track played once a month with a trained skill.

## How to get there

- Sit for ten minutes daily for the first two weeks. Same time, same spot, no exceptions and no app dependency once the technique is learned.
- Pick one anchor — breath, body sensation, or sound — and use only that one for the first ninety days. Do not chase every technique that gets mentioned.
- When you notice wandering, name it in one word — "thinking," "planning" — and return to the anchor. That return is the rep; there is no such thing as a bad session.
- Extend duration by five minutes every two weeks until forty-five minutes is unremarkable.
- Sit through discomfort once a week on purpose: an itch unscratched, a position unshifted, a full session with no music or guidance.
- Book one extended sit — a day retreat at minimum — within the next twelve months, and repeat it annually.

## Measures

- Practice log: twenty or more minutes daily for the last ninety days, with no more than three missed days.
- At least three sits of forty-five minutes or longer in the last thirty days.
- One extended retreat or silent day of six or more hours completed in the last twelve months, dated.
- Self-report: in the last week, at least one full session sat without checking a phone, app, or timer before it ended.

## Canon

- **Mindfulness in Plain English**, Bhante Henepola Gunaratana — the clearest working instruction manual for the technique this standard is built on.
- **The Mind Illuminated**, Culadasa (John Yates) — a detailed, stage-based map of attention training from beginner to advanced sitting practice.
- **Wherever You Go, There You Are**, Jon Kabat-Zinn — the secular framing of mindfulness that made sustained daily practice accessible outside a monastery.
- **Altered Traits**, Daniel Goleman & Richard J. Davidson — the research evidence for what sustained meditation practice actually changes, separating the durable trait from the temporary state.
