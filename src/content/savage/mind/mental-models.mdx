## What it looks like

A model is a compressed piece of the world that lets you predict it without living through every instance first. The savage keeps a working set — inversion, opportunity cost, second-order effects, base rates, margin of safety, Hanlon's razor, the map is not the territory — and knows which one fits which shape of problem, because the model has been used, not just read about.

The models are not trivia. They are load-bearing. Before a decision above a stated threshold, the savage runs it through the two or three models that apply and writes down what each one says. When a model produces a different answer than the gut, the model wins by default, and the disagreement gets logged so it can be checked later.

Checklists cover the mistakes that recur under pressure, not the ones that are interesting. Hiring, negotiating, a major purchase, a health decision, a hard conversation, shipping something to the public — each has a written checklist built from actual past failures, and the savage runs it every time, especially when experienced enough to feel it is unnecessary.

The ceiling reads: a maintained list of twenty-five or more working models with a one-line trigger for each — the situation that should call it to mind — a written checklist for every recurring high-stakes decision in the savage's life, and a log of model use that shows the model changed the outcome, not just decorated it.

## The gap

The gap looks like collecting frameworks on a slide and using none of them under real pressure. It looks like "I've read Charlie Munger" and a decision made on vibes twenty minutes later. It looks like skipping the checklist because this time is different — it never is. It looks like a model reached for after the decision, to justify it, instead of before, to make it.

## How to get there

- Build the list. Twenty-five models minimum, one line each: name, what it says, the situation that should trigger it. Keep it somewhere seen weekly.
- Write one checklist for the highest-stakes recurring decision in your life first. Base it on your last three failures in that category, not a template found online.
- Before any decision above your stated threshold, name the two models that apply out loud or on paper before deciding, not after.
- When the model and the gut disagree, follow the model and write down the disagreement with a date. Review it in six months.
- Add a model to the list only after using it on a real decision, never after only reading about it (see MND·02).
- Prune quarterly. A model that has not fired in a year is not on the list for free.

## Measures

- Model list exists, has twenty-five or more entries with triggers, and was reviewed or edited in the last ninety days.
- Written checklists exist for every recurring decision category you can name that carries real cost when done badly.
- Decision log shows at least five entries in the last year where a model changed the call, with the date and the outcome once known.
- Ask yourself the last three decisions above your threshold: for each, name the model applied before deciding, not after.

## Canon

- **Poor Charlie's Almanack**, Charles T. Munger — the primary source for multidisciplinary mental models used as a practical decision toolkit.
- **The Great Mental Models**, Shane Parrish & the Farnam Street team — an organized working reference for the models that recur across fields.
- **The Checklist Manifesto**, Atul Gawande — the case for the checklist as a discipline that catches expertise's own blind spots.
- **Seeking Wisdom: From Darwin to Munger**, Peter Bevelin — a synthesis of cross-disciplinary models applied directly to judgement and decision-making.
