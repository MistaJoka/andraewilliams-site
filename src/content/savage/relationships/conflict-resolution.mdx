## What it looks like

Conflict is not a sign the relationship is broken; avoiding it is. The savage treats disagreement as normal maintenance and argues it the way a professional argues a case: at the issue, not the person, toward a resolution both sides can name.

The rules are fixed before they are needed. No contempt — no eye-rolling, name-calling, or mockery; it is the single strongest predictor of a relationship's end, and the savage treats it as a hard line, not a bad day. No stonewalling — silence is not a strategy, and "I need a minute" comes with a time to return, never an open-ended exit. No kitchen-sinking — the fight stays on the subject that started it, not every grievance since last year.

Repair happens on a deadline. A rupture — a fight, a broken promise, a hurt landed by accident — gets a direct conversation within 48 hours, an actual apology that names the specific harm rather than "sorry you feel that way," and a repair on both sides when both sides own part of it. Resolution means an agreement either party could restate accurately, not a ceasefire that leaves the issue live under the surface.

## The gap

The gap looks like a fight "won" that nobody actually resolved — the loser goes quiet, the winner claims victory, and the same argument returns in six weeks wearing a different subject line. It looks like silent treatment mistaken for maturity. It looks like an apology that reopens the wound: "I'm sorry, but you—". It looks like conflict avoided so completely that resentment does the talking instead.

## How to get there

- Agree the ground rules before the next fight, not during it: no contempt, no stonewalling past an agreed return time, one subject per conversation.
- Use a hard stop and restart. If either side is flooded — heart racing, unable to listen — call a twenty-minute break with a fixed return time, every time.
- Apologize in a full sentence that names the specific harm and what changes, and land it within 48 hours of the rupture.
- Close every disagreement with a spoken or written agreement both sides can restate. If you cannot both state the resolution the same way, it is not resolved.
- Track the pattern. A fight that recurs three times is not a fight, it is an unmet need — name the need directly instead of refighting the argument.

## Measures

- Ruptures with a partner (see REL·01), family member, or close friend in the last ninety days: each one repaired within 48 hours, or logged as still open with a reason.
- Contempt — mockery, name-calling, eye-rolling — used in the last ninety days: zero. Audit honestly.
- Recurring arguments, same subject three-plus times in a year, identified and named as an unmet need, in writing.
- Longest stretch of unresolved silence with someone in the inner circle after a conflict in the last year: under 72 hours.
- Apologies given in the last quarter that named the specific harm, not a generic "sorry": count them against total conflicts.

## Canon

- **Difficult Conversations**, Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton & Sheila Heen — the Harvard Negotiation Project's framework for having the hard conversation instead of avoiding it or losing it.
- **Nonviolent Communication**, Marshall B. Rosenberg — a concrete method for stating a grievance without attack and hearing one without defense.
- **Crucial Conversations**, Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan & Al Switzler — how to stay in a high-stakes conversation instead of going silent or going aggressive.
- **Getting to Yes**, Roger Fisher & William Ury — negotiating toward an agreement both sides actually hold, not a concession forced on the weaker party.
