## What it looks like

The savage is never the most capable person they know. There are two or three people ten to twenty years further down the road — in the craft, in money, in marriage, in leadership — whose advice is sought before major decisions and acted on. These relationships are earned, not requested cold. They are kept alive by asking good questions, reporting back on what happened, and never wasting their time.

It looks like being a good mentee: arriving with a specific question and the work already done, taking notes, closing the loop within a month on what you did with the advice. Mentors keep investing in people who show returns.

It looks like mentoring downward with the same seriousness. The savage has two to four people they are actively raising — a junior colleague, a younger relative, someone who asked for help and meant it. They get regular time, honest feedback, introductions, and a standard set high enough to be worth reaching. The goal is that they pass you. The savage treats that as the win, not a threat.

## The gap

The gap looks like "I don't really have a mentor" at thirty-five. It looks like one coffee with a senior person, never followed up. It looks like asking for advice and arguing with it. It looks like being asked to mentor and delivering a monthly cancelled calendar invite. It looks like hoarding knowledge because a rising junior feels like a threat.

## How to get there

- Name your advisors. Write down two or three people and the domain each one covers. Where a seat is empty, list three candidates and start the relationship this quarter by asking one specific, well-prepared question.
- Report back. Every piece of advice taken gets a follow-up within thirty days: what you did, what happened.
- Take on mentees explicitly. Agree a cadence — every two to four weeks, thirty to sixty minutes — and hold it as firmly as a client meeting.
- Set mentees a standard and a next step every session. Vague encouragement is not mentoring.
- Make introductions freely. Your network is a resource you hand down.

## Measures

- Advisors named in writing, each with a substantive conversation logged in the last ninety days.
- Every piece of advice taken in the last year has a dated report-back. Count the gaps.
- Mentees named, two to four, each with a held session in the last thirty days.
- At least one mentee in the last five years who has moved past you in some measurable way — title, income, skill — and would credit you if asked.
- Introductions made on behalf of others in the last quarter: at least three, logged.
