## What it looks like

Beyond the partner, the family, and the small inner circle (see REL·03) sits a wider tribe — the community that would notice if you vanished and show up if you called. The savage builds this deliberately: a running group, a professional guild, a faith community, a neighbourhood, a hobby scene — somewhere with recurring faces and a name for what it is.

Membership is earned by contribution before extraction. The savage volunteers for the unglamorous roles — organizing, showing up early, running the thing nobody wants to run — before asking the community for anything. Reciprocity is real and tracked loosely: help given roughly balances help received over years, not per transaction.

It looks like showing up for people two rings out from the inner circle — the acquaintance's fundraiser, the colleague's book launch, the neighbour's house move — because a community is built from a hundred small appearances, not five large ones. It looks like introductions made freely across the network, connecting people who should know each other with nothing in it for you.

## The gap

The gap looks like a professional network that is a spreadsheet of names who would not recognize your voice on the phone. It looks like showing up only to events that serve you directly — the ones with a boss watching, or a lead in the room. It looks like a neighbourhood lived in for a decade where nobody knows your name. It looks like every RSVP declined until the day you need the community and discover it does not know you well enough to answer.

## How to get there

- Join one recurring group with a fixed cadence — weekly, monthly — and attend for a year before judging whether it is the right one.
- Take an unglamorous role in it within the first six months: organizer, treasurer, the person who opens up. Visibility without contribution is not membership.
- Say yes to two-rings-out invitations — acquaintances, not just close friends — at a rate of at least one a month.
- Make three introductions a month between people in your network who would benefit from knowing each other, with nothing asked in return.
- Keep a loose ledger. Once a quarter, ask honestly whether you have given more than you have taken over the last year. Adjust if not.

## Measures

- Recurring community or group attended with a fixed cadence for the last twelve months: named, with an attendance rate.
- Unglamorous or organizing role held in a community within the last year: yes or no.
- Two-rings-out events, not immediate family, not inner circle, attended in the last ninety days: count them.
- Introductions made in the last quarter with no benefit to yourself: at least nine, three a month.
- Community members outside the inner circle who could name a specific way you helped them in the last year: at least three, named.

## Canon

- **Bowling Alone**, Robert D. Putnam — the decline of American community and the mechanism by which social capital is built or lost.
- **Palaces for the People**, Eric Klinenberg — the case for shared physical and social spaces as the infrastructure real community is built on.
- **Give and Take**, Adam Grant — the evidence that people who contribute before extracting build the strongest, most durable networks over time.
- **The Art of Community**, Charles H. Vogl — seven concrete principles, drawn from ancient and modern groups alike, for building belonging on purpose.
