## What it looks like

Strength is the foundation everything else in the Body domain stands on. The savage trains it as a permanent practice, not a phase. Compound lifts are the spine of the program — squat, hinge, press, pull — and they are performed with full range of motion and technique that would pass a coach's eye.

The numbers are not vanity. They are a floor below which you do not fall. For a trained adult, the ceiling reads: bodyweight bench, 1.5× bodyweight squat, 2× bodyweight deadlift, twelve strict pull-ups, a one-minute dead hang held with room to spare. These are adjusted for age and frame, never excused by them.

Strength shows in daily life before it shows on a bar. You carry everything in one trip. You move furniture without a second thought. You pick up a child, a bag of concrete, a stalled person, without bracing for it.

## The gap

The gap looks like "I used to lift." It looks like a program that changes every three weeks because the last one got hard. It looks like avoiding the lifts that expose weakness — no squats because of knees that were never rehabbed, no pull-ups because zero is embarrassing. It looks like strong arms and a weak back, or a big bench and a deadlift nobody has seen.

## How to get there

- Pick one proven linear or periodised program and run it for a year without modification. Novices add weight every session; intermediates every week; everyone logs every set.
- Train three to four strength sessions per week, forty-five to seventy-five minutes, compound lifts first, accessories second.
- Technique before load. Film the lifts. Fix what the footage shows.
- Eat enough protein to recover — roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kilogram of bodyweight per day — and sleep enough to adapt (see BDY·04).
- Rehab every injury to full function. A nagging shoulder is not a personality trait.

## Measures

- Current 1RM or 3RM on squat, hinge, press, pull, written down and dated within the last ninety days.
- Strict pull-ups: count them, monthly.
- Training log exists and has no unexplained gaps longer than two weeks in the last year.
- Zero movements avoided because of an unrehabbed injury.

## Canon

- **Starting Strength**, Mark Rippetoe — the definitive case for barbell compound lifts as the base of a lifelong strength practice.
- **Practical Programming for Strength Training**, Mark Rippetoe & Andy Baker — the programming logic behind linear progression and why it eventually has to change.
- **Science and Practice of Strength Training**, Vladimir Zatsiorsky & William Kraemer — the research foundation for how strength adapts and how load, volume, and intensity actually work.
- **The New Rules of Lifting**, Lou Schuler & Alwyn Cosgrove — a readable bridge from bodybuilding-era thinking to modern strength and conditioning.
