## What it looks like

Adaptation happens between sessions, not during them. The savage plans recovery as part of the program, not as a reaction to breaking down.

The ceiling reads: a planned deload every four to eight weeks, taken on schedule whether or not it feels needed — volume or intensity cut by a third to a half for a week, movement kept, ego left at the door. Fatigue is tracked, not guessed: bar speed, grip strength, resting heart rate, mood, and motivation are watched for the pattern that says the next hard week will not go well.

Injury is managed as a project with an endpoint, not a condition lived with. Pain that changes how you move gets assessed within a week, not a year. A rehab plan exists, has a timeline, and is followed as strictly as the main program — return to full load is the finish line, not "good enough to avoid it."

Modalities are used for what they actually do. Heat loosens tissue and eases soreness before or after work. Cold water immersion blunts inflammation and perceived soreness — and is timed away from the hours right after a hypertrophy session, where it can blunt the adaptation too (see BDY·01). Massage, foam rolling and mobility work manage tissue quality and feel. Active recovery — an easy walk, a light spin, a swim — clears the legs faster than a day on the couch. None of it is a substitute for sleep (see BDY·04), which is still the primary recovery tool.

## The gap

The gap looks like training through pain until a niggle becomes a tear. It looks like never deloading because the log looks better with an unbroken string of PRs, followed by an unplanned two-month layoff when something finally gives. It looks like an ice bath posted for an audience and skipped when nobody's watching, or a foam roller bought once and never used again. It looks like "I'll rest when I'm dead" said by someone whose numbers have been stalled for a year.

## How to get there

- Build the deload into the program before you start it — a fixed week every four to eight weeks, calendared, not decided in the moment.
- Track one fatigue marker weekly: resting heart rate, grip strength, or a simple readiness score. Two bad weeks in a row triggers an extra deload, not more volume.
- Get pain assessed within seven days of it changing how you move. Write the rehab plan with a physio or qualified clinician, with a target date for full load.
- Keep one heat and one cold protocol in rotation — sauna or hot bath weekly, cold exposure two to three times a week — timed away from the two hours after a muscle-building session.
- Schedule active recovery on days off from hard training: twenty to forty minutes, low intensity, movement for its own sake.
- Log every injury and its resolution in one place. Review it before starting a new training block.

## Measures

- Deloads taken on schedule for the last six cycles, logged with dates.
- Zero movements currently modified or avoided due to an unresolved injury.
- Any pain lasting more than two weeks has a written assessment and rehab plan on file.
- One fatigue marker tracked weekly for the last twelve weeks with no unexplained gaps.
- Active recovery sessions logged on non-training days, at least once a week on average.

## Canon

- **The Recovery Code**, Steve Long & Kris Gethin — a training-side breakdown of deloads, fatigue management, and periodised recovery.
- **Peak Performance**, Brad Stulberg & Steve Magness — the stress-plus-rest model that makes deliberate recovery, not just hard work, the lever for growth.
- **Supertraining**, Yuri Verkhoshansky & Mel Siff — the sports-science reference behind periodisation and planned unloading phases.
- **What Doesn't Kill Us**, Scott Carney — an investigative account of cold exposure and voluntary stress that grounds the case for heat and cold protocols in something more than trend.
